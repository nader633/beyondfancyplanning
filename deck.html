Case One  ·  The Failure

A $1.7 million toilet

The plumbing was already in the ground. The restroom had been in Noe Valley Town Square's site plan since 2016, postponed only for lack of funding.

In October 2022, the California Department of Parks and Recreation announced $1.7 million for a single restroom at Noe Valley Town Square. Projected timeline: two years. Projected opening: 2025.

Where did the money go?

Custom design and PG&E infrastructure demands
Construction costs up 20–30% in two years
Living wages with benefits for workers
Planning, permitting, review, outreach, management

Every one defensible on its own. Together: $1.7 million, two years — for one toilet.

What actually went wrong

Not one step. Four things failed at once.

01
Nobody defended what it was for. A line item on a work plan — and line items lose to process every time.
02
Everyone had veto power. Nobody had championship. Too many parties, none with authority to move the work.
03
Every value turned up, except time. Engagement, wages, transparency at maximum. Time turned to zero.
04
Prevention systems prevented the work itself. Too many, all pointing at one project, with no way to say enough.

The toilet is a project, not a program. Each failure is a symptom of missing scaffolding.

A program would have carried its purpose forward.
A program would have named an authority to move the work.
A program would have weighed time against the other values.
A program would have built delivery systems, not just prevention.
Case Two  ·  The Contrast

A $21 million accelerator

Colorado took $50M of an EPA climate grant and built the Local IMPACT Accelerator — passing funding down to cities, counties, and Tribal governments to adopt local climate policy.

Fort Collins is adopting building performance standards. Winter Park is building a thermal energy network with net-zero bus stops. Adams County won $2M for renewable-energy land use.

What Colorado did differently

01
Four sectors, one product: adopted policy. Buildings, land, transport, waste. Ordinances you can count — not moonshots.
02
Put the state in the middle, lowering barriers. Regional Coordinators help draft applications; Letters of Intent give an early signal.
03
Cut the match for those least able to pay. Zero for low-income and Tribal governments, 5% for everyone else — said openly.
04
Built delivery infrastructure into the program. The Coordinators and the Letter of Intent were systems, designed before the first application.
The Pattern

What every delivering program shares

44 practitioners. Four countries. One question — what actually made your program work? Four things kept showing up.

The Four Ps

Purpose
A direction someone is willing to defend, even before the details are settled.
People
The parties whose presence, engagement, and authority decide whether work moves.
Priorities
The value choices about what the program will actually spend on — honestly reckoned.
Platform
The infrastructure underneath that either lets the work happen or quietly ensures it doesn't.
The aha

A program is a narrative for change.

A story that moves a whole community to act, and to keep acting, for good. Look at what a lasting story carries, and you see the four again.

The framework is really a test

Does it know what good it is for?
Does it hold the whole community?
Do its choices match its claims?
Will it survive the people who started it?
