A Framework Essay

Beyond Fancy Planning

What makes public programs actually deliver, and what happens when they don't.

Synthesizing insights from 44 practitioners in program design and delivery, across four countries.

Case One · The Failure

A $1.7 million toilet

For years, residents of San Francisco's Noe Valley pushed for a public toilet at Noe Valley Town Square. The plumbing was already in the ground. The town square had opened in 2016 and the restroom was in the original site plan, but it had been postponed for lack of funding.

Where it standsThe Noe Valley toilet opened in April 2024. Final cost, after two private donations: around $300,000 — less than a fifth of the original estimate.

In October 2022, the city announced the plan. The California Department of Parks and Recreation had allocated $1.7 million for a single restroom at Noe Valley Town Square. The projected construction timeline was two years. The projected opening date was 2025.

One toilet.  $1.7 million.  Two years to build.

Where did the money go?

Recreation and Parks laid it out for the San Francisco Chronicle. The cost estimate reflected several factors. There was the custom design, and the demands PG&E made on the infrastructure. Construction costs had risen 20 to 30 percent over the previous two years. The project included living wages with benefits for the workers. And it had to move through the standard sequence any city project requires: planning, drawing, permitting, review, public outreach, and construction management.

Every one of those is defensible on its own. Together, they added up to $1.7 million for a single toilet, over a two-year timeline. For one toilet.

State Assemblyman Matt Haney called it "inexplicable." The story went national in October 2022. The scheduled ribbon-cutting was canceled. Two private companies stepped in with a prefabricated donation worth $425,000. Governor Newsom paused the state grant and released it later only on the condition that the city build more than one toilet with the money.

What actually went wrong

Author's noteI wrote about this story earlier this year in The Impact Angle. Several people reached out afterward, defending each step of the process — the review, the labor rules, the permitting sequence. Each one held. None explained the whole.

None of the individual steps explained the core problem. Why did a single toilet in a park need a $1.7 million estimate and a two-year timeline — especially with the plumbing already in the ground? The reason isn't any one step. It's that four things went wrong at the same time.

  1. Nobody was defending what the program was actually for. There was no compelling narrative that anyone was carrying forward. It was a line item on a work plan, and line items lose to process every time.
  2. Too many parties had a role, and none had the authority to move the work. Recreation and Parks, PG&E, permit reviewers, design review, public outreach. Everyone had veto power. Nobody had championship.
  3. Every value the city holds got turned up, except time. Community engagement, living wages, transparency, design review — all at maximum volume. Time turned down to zero. The two-year timeline was simply what the process demanded.
  4. The systems built to prevent bad outcomes prevented the work itself. Not because the systems are bad — because there were too many of them, all pointing at one small project, with no mechanism for anyone to say enough.

One more thing to name. The Noe Valley toilet is a project. Not a program. That is the point. San Francisco has no coherent public-infrastructure delivery program that this project could sit inside. What it has is a collection of departments, each doing its bureaucratic function, none carrying responsibility for the whole.

A program would have carried the toilet's purpose forward. A program would have named an authority who could move the work. A program would have weighed time against the other values. A program would have built delivery systems alongside prevention systems.

The toilet had to make its way alone, inside a bureaucracy whose default job is to slow things down. The pattern this essay is about is what happens at the program level. When programs get the Four Ps right, projects like this one move. When programs get them wrong or absent, projects like this one stall.

Case Two · The Contrast

A $21 million accelerator

Now a program trying to do the opposite — a more plausible example, from a different state. In July 2024, the EPA awarded Colorado a $129 million Climate Pollution Reduction Grant, implementation money from the Inflation Reduction Act meant to help states cut greenhouse gas emissions.

The mechanismColorado took $50 million and built the Local IMPACT Accelerator: pass the funding down to cities, counties, and Tribal governments so they can adopt local climate policies.

The state ran the first round in about seven months. By January 2026, Colorado had awarded $21.6 million to 17 projects. Fort Collins is adopting building performance standards. Winter Park is developing a thermal energy network with net-zero bus stops. Adams County received $2 million for renewable energy land use policy.

Seven months.  $21.6 million.  17 projects.

Not one of them glamorous. Colorado's projects are still early — we don't yet know whether the ordinances will pass or the buildings will be retrofitted. What we do know is what the state did at the design stage.

What Colorado did differently

Colorado made four design choices, deliberately.

  1. They picked four sectors and one product: adopted policy. Buildings, land use, transportation, waste. Not "climate action" in the abstract, not moonshots — ordinances, codes, and training programs. Things you can count.
  2. They put the state in the middle, and spent its position lowering barriers. Regional Coordinators, real humans assigned by region, help local governments prepare applications. Letters of Intent get an early signal, so a town with one planner doesn't burn months on a long shot.
  3. They cut the local match for the communities least able to pay it. Most grants require the recipient to put up matching money as proof of commitment. Colorado set the match at zero for low-income communities and Tribal governments, 5 percent for everyone else — treating the match as a barrier, not a discipline, and saying so openly.
  4. They built the delivery infrastructure into the program itself. The Regional Coordinators are a system. The Letter of Intent is a system. Both were designed before the first application arrived, not bolted on after.

Four choices, made together.

The Pattern

What every delivering program shares

Over the past year, I spent time with 44 practitioners who design, run, and evaluate public-serving programs — state officials, foundation leaders, city planners, nonprofit directors, academics, consultants who move between them. In four countries. I asked all of them a version of the same question: what actually made your program work?

SourcesThis essay also draws on more than two decades of my own work in program design and evaluation, and on recent research on why some programs deliver and others don't. The interviews gave the pattern its shape.

Four things kept showing up. I have come to call them the Four Ps.

Purpose
A direction the work is going that someone is willing to defend, even when the details are not yet settled.
People
The parties whose presence, engagement, and authority determine whether the work moves.
Priorities
The value choices about what the program will actually spend on — honestly reckoned, rather than uniformly claimed.
Platform
The infrastructure of resources, capacity, and systems underneath that either lets the work happen or quietly ensures it does not.

The first three shape what the program tries to do. Platform sits underneath as the foundation that lets the first three work. And when these four came together, something clicked for me. It took me back.

In my twenties I was fascinated by dreams — by what they were and why we have them. That curiosity pulled me into philosophy. I read Nietzsche first. Then, chasing dream interpretation, I found Jung, and through Jung a whole world opened up: how stories move between generations, how the unconscious works, why some stories survive and keep shaping people long after the teller is gone.

Looking at the four Ps, that old fascination lit back up. Because these are the same four things a lasting story carries — the ones a grandmother passes to her grandchildren, the ones a community keeps telling across generations, because they changed something real. That was the aha.

A program is a narrative for change. It is a story that moves a whole community to act, and to keep acting, for good.

The good programs are the ones telling a story people carry forward and live. Look at what a lasting story carries, and you see the four again. Such a story is always about something — a direction, a good it is driving toward. That is Purpose. It has people in it: characters it happens to, and a community it is told by and for; in the stories that last, the good reaches the good. That is People. It chooses what it dwells on — it cannot tell everything, so what it spends its time on shows what it truly values. That is Priorities. And it outlives its first telling: new voices carry it, it holds together as it passes hand to hand, it still means something under new conditions. That is Platform — what lets the good outlast the teller.

So the framework is really a test. Show me your program's story.

Does it know what good it is for?

Does it hold the whole community?

Do its choices match its claims?

Will it survive the people who started it?

San Francisco proposed spending $1.7 million and two years to build one toilet because it missed all four. Colorado moved $21.6 million to 17 projects in about seven months because it built for all four from the start. Elaborate strategic plans sit on shelves for the same reason a single Noe Valley toilet was projected to cost $1.7 million: they get one or two of the four right and miss the others. Programs that actually deliver for communities hold all four together.

In the sections that follow, I'll walk through each one. The practitioners will do the teaching. I'll point at the patterns.

Let's start with Purpose.
