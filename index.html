What makes public programs actually deliver, and what happens when they don't.

44 practitioners  ·  four countries  ·  one question
↓ Scroll

Elaborate strategic plans sit on shelves. Meanwhile, a single public toilet can take two years and cost $1.7 million.

Over the past year I asked 44 practitioners one question: what actually made your program work? The same pattern kept coming up. Let's start where it breaks.

01
The Failure

A $1.7 million toilet

The plumbing was already in the ground. The restroom had been in Noe Valley Town Square's plan since 2016.

In October 2022, the California Department of Parks and Recreation announced $1.7 million for a single restroom. Projected timeline: two years. Projected opening: 2025.

One restroom · Noe Valley Town Square

State Assemblyman Matt Haney called it "inexplicable." The story went national. The ribbon-cutting was canceled.

Every one defensible on its own.

But none of it explained the core problem.

Four things went wrong at the same time.

01
Nobody defended what it was for. It was a line item on a work plan, and line items lose to process every time.
02
Everyone had veto power. Nobody had championship. Too many parties, none with the authority to move the work.
03
Every value turned up, except time. Engagement, wages, transparency at maximum. Time turned to zero.
04
Prevention systems prevented the work itself. Too many, all pointing at one project, with no way to say enough.

Four things
at once.

The toilet is a project, not a program. Every failure here comes from missing scaffolding. A program would have held it together. A program:

carried its purpose forward.
named an authority who could move the work.
weighed time against the other values.
built delivery systems, not just prevention.
02
The Contrast

A $21 million accelerator

Colorado took $50M of an EPA climate grant and passed it down to cities, counties, and Tribal governments.

7 mo
first round
$21.6M
awarded
17
projects

Four design choices, made deliberately.

01
Four sectors, one product: adopted policy. Ordinances you can count, not moonshots.
02
Put the state in the middle, lowering barriers. Regional Coordinators help draft applications; Letters of Intent give an early signal.
03
Cut the match for those least able to pay. Zero for low-income and Tribal governments, and they said so up front.
04
Built delivery infrastructure into the program. Designed before the first application arrived, not bolted on after.

Four choices,
made together.

03
The Pattern

What every delivering program shares

Four things kept showing up. I came to call them the Four Ps.

PURPOSE
A direction someone will defend, even before the details are settled.
PEOPLE
The people whose presence, engagement, and authority decide whether work moves.
PRIORITIES
What the program actually spends on. You can't raise everything, so something has to give.
PLATFORM
The infrastructure underneath. The other three stand on it.
The aha

A program is a narrative for change.

A story that moves a whole community to act, and to keep acting. A lasting story carries the same four things.

The ones a grandmother passes to her grandchildren.

The stories a community keeps telling across generations, because they changed something real.

Why a story

Look at what a lasting story carries, and you see the four again.

It is always about something.
PURPOSE
A direction, a good it is driving toward.
It has people in it.
PEOPLE
Characters it happens to, and a community it is told by and for.
It chooses what it dwells on.
PRIORITIES
What it spends its time on shows what it truly values.
It outlives its first telling.
PLATFORM
New voices carry it hand to hand, and it still means something under new conditions.

The good programs are telling a story people carry forward and live.

So the framework is really a test. Show me your program's story.

Does it know what good it is for?

Does it hold the whole community?

Do its choices match its claims?

Will it survive the people who started it?

The same four tests, opposite results
